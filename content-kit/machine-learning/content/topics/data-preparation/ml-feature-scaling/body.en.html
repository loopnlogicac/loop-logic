Taka can drown out rooms

Tuktuk searches for a home like Mina's drawing. The drawing records rooms and a monthly budget. A one-room difference barely moves the robot, while a hundred-taka difference sends it running. Nana asks why a small money change matters more than an entire room. Tuktuk has been adding raw numeric gaps without thinking about their units.

Distance calculations see numbers, not the importance of their units. Large numeric ranges can dominate smaller ones. Scaling changes how features contribute.

You will compare range scaling with standardisation, fit their values on training rows, and watch an actual nearest-neighbour choice change.

Give both axes a deliberate measuring stick

Min-max scaling maps a training column's minimum to zero and maximum to one. Standardisation subtracts a training mean and divides by its standard deviation. A scaler stores these fitted values so later rows receive the same conversion.

Subtract an offset, divide by a spread

For x, the training minimum is 1 and range is 6 minus 1, or 5. For y, the minimum is 100 and range is 900 minus 100, or 800. A transformed value is its original value minus the minimum, divided by the range.

The query becomes (0.4,0.875). Row 2 becomes (0.2,1); row 4 becomes (0.4,0.725). Their transformed distances are about 0.236 and 0.150. Row 4 is now nearer. The original y gap no longer overwhelms the difference along x. Changing scale has changed what similar means.

For standardisation, use z=(value minus mean) divided by standard deviation. Here z is the converted value. StandardScaler uses population variance, dividing the squared-gap sum by n. It does not use pandas' default sample standard deviation.

A constant column has no spread. Both library scalers effectively use divisor one for such a column after subtracting its offset. All its training values become zero. This avoids division by zero without inventing information.

The study example uses KNN, which predicts by voting among nearby training rows. The pipeline first fills missing measurements, then standardises them, then learns the neighbour lookup.

KNN and SVM depend on distances or margins. Gradient-based training is often more stable with comparable feature scales. Ordinary decision trees split one feature at a time; positive linear rescaling preserves possible row partitions, so trees generally do not require scaling.

Fit once on the right partition

  • Fitting a scaler before splitting reveals held-out extrema or means. Split first and fit on training rows.
  • Refitting separately on test data makes identical measurements mean different coordinates. Call transform with the fitted training scaler.
  • Assuming standardisation removes outliers confuses scale with validity. Extreme observations still need investigation.
  • Normalising a target and forgetting to invert predictions changes the reported units. Keep target and feature transformations explicit.

Optional: scaling chooses a distance geometry

After division by a feature scale s, that feature's squared distance contribution is its original squared gap divided by s squared. Scaling therefore weights coordinates; it is not a neutral cosmetic change. If one feature matters more for a domain reason, a validated weighting may be justified.

Min-max scaling is sensitive to extreme training values because they set the range. Mean and standard deviation are also sensitive to extremes. No automatic scaling choice guarantees the most useful similarity measure.

One fitted ruler for future data

  • Standardisation uses a training mean and population spread.
  • Constant columns need an explicit safe divisor.
  • Reuse fitted values for validation, test and future queries.
