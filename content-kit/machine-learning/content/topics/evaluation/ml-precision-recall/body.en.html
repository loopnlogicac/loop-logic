Accuracy, precision, recall and F1 ask different questions about a classifier. Their denominators reveal those questions. A useful report chooses measures for the decision being made and keeps the underlying class counts visible.

Precision asks how many predicted positives are really positive. Recall asks how many actual positives were found. F1 combines those two rates using their harmonic mean. Accuracy instead asks how many examples of either class were correct.

In the table, five examples were flagged positive, and three are correct flags. Four examples were actually positive, and three were found. The same numerator appears in precision and recall, but their populations differ. Naming the denominator is often more helpful than memorising a formula.

Nana's all-fresh rule has no predicted positives and misses the only actual positive. Its 99% accuracy is real arithmetic, but it answers a broader question dominated by fresh cards. It does not establish useful detection of spoiled cards.

Calculate four measures without changing the counts

The twelve-case table contains nine exact matches: six true negatives and three true positives. Accuracy is nine divided by twelve, or 0.75. Every case contributes to that denominator, including the large negative group.

Precision is three divided by the five predicted positives, giving 0.6. Recall is three divided by the four actual positives, giving 0.75. F1 is twice precision times recall divided by their sum, about 0.667. Equivalently, use twice TP divided by twice TP plus FP plus FN.

F1 is not the arithmetic average of precision and recall. Its harmonic form penalises a large imbalance between them. It also ignores true negatives, so it cannot describe every important consequence of a decision. Choose the measure because its question fits the application, not because its name sounds comprehensive.

state a policy for any zero denominator

When no positives are predicted, precision has a zero denominator. The ratio is mathematically undefined. The reference code uses an explicit zero reporting convention to match zero_division=0 in scikit-learn. Another report might display unavailable instead; preserve counts and explain the choice.

If no actual positives were evaluated, recall is also undefined before a convention. A printed zero in that situation does not mean observed positives were all missed. It means there was no positive denominator. Distinguish missing evidence from measured poor performance.

A decision constraint can guide metric choice

A review queue may require finding at least a stated fraction of actual positives. Under that constraint, precision asks how much unnecessary review remains among the flags. The coding exercise selects precision only after checking the recall requirement, making the policy explicit.

Raising a fixed-score threshold shrinks the predicted-positive set, so recall cannot increase. Precision can rise or fall depending on which true and false positives leave that set. Do not promise a universal precision improvement merely because a cutoff becomes stricter.

Choose thresholds using validation data and the intended error costs. Repeatedly choosing the best-looking final-test F1 adapts the workflow to those test answers. Keep one separate final evaluation for the resulting fixed policy.

Read supports before averaging classes

classification_report lists per-class precision, recall, F1 and support. Support is the number of actual evaluated examples in that class. A small support means a small number of changed answers can move its rate substantially, even when the overall sample looks large.

Macro averaging gives every class equal weight. Support-weighted averaging gives large classes more influence. A strong weighted average can therefore conceal a weak rare class. Keep the individual rows available instead of replacing them with one preferred summary.

Macro F1 averages the class F1 values. It is generally different from taking macro precision and macro recall first and applying the F1 formula afterwards. The order of operations changes the question, so use the library's documented averaging definition.

Always retain the denominator size beside a rate. One missed case among four actual positives moves recall by a quarter, while one among four hundred moves it much less. Equal printed percentages can therefore rest on very different amounts of evidence.

Choosing F1 as a universal objective

F1 ignores true negatives and does not encode every application's unequal error costs. Compare relevant measures and a decision policy rather than assuming one summary answers every question.

Optional: an averaging rule is part of the metric

With positive denominators, precision P=TP/(TP+FP) and recall R=TP/(TP+FN). Their harmonic mean is 2PR/(P+R), reducing to 2TP/(2TP+FP+FN). The reference implementation specifies zero for otherwise undefined precision, recall or F1 denominators.

Metrics are estimates on the evaluated population. Class prevalence changes precision even when some conditional error rates remain stable. Report the sample design, class supports and chosen threshold alongside results, especially when comparing collections with different category mixtures.
