Choose the settings without peeking at the final answers

Mina tries several neighbour counts for Tuktuk. Nana asks whether she chose the winner because it worked on practice cards or because she kept checking the final exam cards. Rafi separates choosing a setting from measuring the chosen setting fairly.

Hyperparameter tuning compares candidate workflows. Each candidate is fitted using training data and measured using validation evidence. A final test answers a later question: how does the fixed choice behave on examples that did not guide selection?

You will read a real validation curve, enumerate a small grid and compare a seeded random search. The search method should have a clear space, budget, scoring rule and fitting boundary.

A fitted value differs from a chosen setting

A parameter is learned while fitting a candidate, such as a logistic coefficient. A hyperparameter is a setting chosen outside that ordinary fit, such as neighbour count or tree depth. A grid search tries every combination from listed setting choices.

A validation curve changes one setting and plots training and validation performance. Its x-axis is a model choice, unlike a learning curve's training-data size. A strong training score is not enough when the candidate is sensitive or memorises examples.

The tiny KNN example keeps six training cards and four validation cards separate. Small requested counts classify all validation cards correctly. Counts exceeding the training set are clamped, making uniform global ties and lower validation accuracy. These are actual predictions, not a curve drawn to look educational.

Spend a search budget explicitly

Random search samples candidate combinations instead of visiting every grid point. It can explore a large space with a fixed number of trials, especially when only some dimensions strongly affect performance. A fixed seed makes the sampled experiment reproducible.

Reproducible does not mean optimal. Four sampled choices can miss the best of twelve. Report the distributions or finite lists, trial count, seed and metric. Keep the same validation discipline as grid search; randomness does not make final-test reuse acceptable.

Finite lists require no additional packages beyond the existing scikit-learn workflow. The example samples four combinations and prints development CV evidence. A search result is conditional on the candidate space and evaluated sample, rather than proof that no better model exists.

A budget should include failed or rejected configurations as part of the experiment record. Check that every candidate is feasible for the smallest training fold, such as a neighbour count no larger than its available rows. Resolve invalid configurations before interpreting the winning score; a silently skipped region of the search space is a different comparison from the one originally described.

Repeatedly checking final-test scores

Choosing maximum training accuracy

Complex models and one-neighbour voting can memorise training examples. Compare held-out behavior and the task's chosen metric instead.

Treating a tiny score gap as certainty

A one-case difference can change a small validation ranking. Inspect class support, repeated evidence and practical costs before claiming a decisive universal winner.

Optional: a selected maximum is optimistic evidence

Searching more candidates increases opportunities to exploit random variation in development scores. The largest observed validation or CV score is therefore selection evidence, not automatically an unbiased final estimate. A separate test or suitable nested validation addresses the assessment of the selection procedure.

The scoring rule is part of the experiment. Accuracy, F1, recall constraints and calibrated probability losses can rank candidates differently. State the positive class, threshold policy and averaging conventions so another reader can reproduce the intended choice.
