Mina tests Tuktuk on two easy fruit cards and gets a perfect score. Nana chooses another pair and the result falls. Rafi suggests giving every card a turn outside training, rather than letting one lucky pair decide the whole story.

Cross-validation repeats fitting across several held-out subsets. Each turn trains a fresh workflow on the remaining rows and measures the excluded part. The collection of scores shows more than one partition can reveal.

You will build deterministic stratified folds, inspect a five-score mean and spread, and place learned preprocessing inside each turn. When those scores help choose a model, keep a separate final evaluation for the chosen workflow.

The ten-card example has five cards per class. Each envelope receives one from each class, so a turn does not accidentally evaluate only apples or only mangoes. That balance helps, but does not guarantee every fold has equally difficult examples.

Training sets overlap across turns. A card held out now can train a later fit. Cross-validation is therefore a repeated development-data procedure, not five permanently untouched final test sets.

A fitted transform can leak without reading labels

A scaler learns means and standard deviations. An imputer learns replacement values, and a text vectorizer learns vocabulary. Fitting any of them on all rows before CV allows a held-out fold to influence its own representation, even if its labels were not used.

Put those transformations in the pipeline passed to cross_val_score or a search procedure. Each cloned workflow then fits on its current training rows and transforms the held-out portion afterwards. Reusing a pre-fitted transformation outside that boundary answers a different, usually more optimistic question.

Label balance is not the only split concern. Related records from the same person, near-duplicate templates or future observations can leak across random folds. Use group-aware or time-respecting partitions when they match the intended prediction setting. Stratification does not automatically enforce those separations.

Suppose one learner contributes several nearly identical measurements. Splitting those rows separately can put the same person’s pattern in training and held-out data. A class-balanced fold can still have that leakage. Group related rows when deployment concerns a new person, and explain that the evaluated unit has changed from a row to a group.

Keep the repeated experiment intact

Training once and scoring five subsets

If the one fit saw every subset, none was held out. Refit the whole learned workflow separately for each turn.

Dropping the weakest fold

All planned folds contribute evidence. Investigate data problems with a documented process rather than deleting an inconvenient result to improve the average.

Calling the standard deviation a confidence interval

Training sets overlap and scores can be dependent. The displayed spread is descriptive; a justified uncertainty estimate needs its own statistical design.

Choosing on CV and claiming an independent CV winner score

Searching many choices can adapt to development folds. Use a separate final test or an appropriate nested design for an independent assessment of selection.

Optional: averaging dependent fits

k-fold CV partitions the index set into k disjoint held-out subsets whose union is all development rows. Each fit uses the complement of one subset. Increasing k changes both training size and dependence among fits; it is not a free guarantee of a better estimate.

Seeded shuffling makes a randomised split reproducible, not representative by itself. The final report should describe grouping, stratification, time assumptions, metric and aggregation. Those choices determine which future-use question the scores can reasonably inform.
