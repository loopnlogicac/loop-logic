Being consistently wrong differs from changing wildly

Mina draws a chalk target for Tuktuk. One set of throws lands close together but far from the centre. Another spreads widely around the target. Nana says that improving the two patterns may require different changes. Rafi connects that picture with models trained on different samples.

Bias describes systematic mismatch, while variance describes sensitivity to the training sample. These ideas help explain why a rigid model can miss a real pattern and a very flexible model can react strongly to small changes. Neither is the same as one observed test error.

You will compare actual polynomial fits across three seeded resamples and read a learning curve that changes data size. Keep the picture as an intuition, then distinguish the finite measurements from the deeper population quantities.

Use the target picture without stretching it too far

A bias is a systematic difference between a model's average prediction and the underlying target relationship. Variance is how much fitted predictions change across possible training samples. A learning curve compares scores as training-set size changes for a chosen workflow.

The tight group away from the dart target illustrates repeatable misdirection. The scattered group illustrates sensitivity. In real data, the true relationship is usually unknown, so you cannot read exact bias from one fitted curve. The analogy helps you ask a question; it does not reveal a hidden population answer.

At one fixed position, suppose three fits predict zero, one and two while the noiseless target is one. Their mean prediction is one and their population variance across these three values is two-thirds. The mean prediction has zero squared error, but individual predictions still average two-thirds squared error. This finite arithmetic separates a central prediction from sensitivity without claiming that three fitted models reveal the full population decomposition.

Change data size while keeping the workflow fixed

A learning curve varies the number of training rows, not the polynomial degree. It evaluates a fixed workflow on training and validation subsets. The study example fits imputation, scaling and ridge inside each fold, then reports mean errors for three training sizes.

A large persistent gap can suggest sensitivity to limited training data, while high close-together errors can suggest underfitting or noise. Those readings require a sensible evaluation design. Different subsets or a changed target population can also move the curves.

The course records are invented. Their learning curve demonstrates how to perform the measurement, not evidence that a particular study habit causes a mark change. Retain the task definition and data source when interpreting a model report.

Avoid replacing evidence with a slogan

Calling any training error bias

Bias concerns expected prediction relative to an underlying relationship. One training score is not that quantity. Describe the observed misfit before making a stronger statistical claim.

Assuming complexity always increases measured variance monotonically

Finite samples, penalties and data geometry affect the result. The lab reports actual three-fit variation rather than forcing a theoretical-looking curve.

Choosing from final-test error

Use validation or cross-validation for complexity decisions. Reserve an independent final check for the fixed workflow.

Removing a small group because its pattern is difficult

Retain valid evidence, inspect support and compare model or sampling improvements. Convenience is not a reason to erase a population's legitimate cases.

Optional: expected error and a finite illustration

Under squared-error assumptions, expected prediction error can be decomposed into squared bias, variance and irreducible noise. Those expectations refer to a data-generating process and possible training samples. A few bootstrap fits do not reveal each population term exactly.

The lab measures variation across three fitted predictions at fixed validation positions. Its regularised normal equations use a small bounded design for transparency; larger numerical problems call for stable least-squares or regularised solvers rather than an unqualified matrix-inverse recipe.

Measure the pattern before choosing a remedy

  • Bias and variance describe different reasons for prediction error.
  • Compare repeated fits at the same query points to see sensitivity.
  • Low training error alone does not establish generalisation.
  • Learning curves vary training size; validation curves vary a model choice.
  • Small bootstrap illustrations do not reveal exact population bias or variance.
