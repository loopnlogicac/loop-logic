Tuktuk moves the string

Tuktuk stretches a string across Nana's fruit cards. It calls everything to the right mango. Mina spots an apple on that side. “Move the string,” she suggests. Tuktuk tries another place and checks the cards again. The string becomes a rule they can improve.

Learning needs a way to compare guesses. Here we count wrong names on known fruit cards. A line making fewer mistakes is better on these cards. That gives Tuktuk a reason to move the string.

The line is only a small model. It looks at size, even though sweetness is also available. Choosing a small model lets you follow every decision. It may miss patterns a richer model could use.

You will try several positions, count wrong guesses and explain the best choice. You will also see why a perfect count on old cards is not a promise about tomorrow.

A fence makes two predictions

A threshold is a dividing value. Our threshold is a size on the horizontal axis. A fruit on the line or to its left is predicted apple. A fruit to its right is predicted mango.

A mistake happens when the predicted name differs from the known label. Count each fruit once. We do not count a mango more heavily because it is sweeter. Later tasks may care differently about different mistakes.

Training is the process of choosing or adjusting a model using examples. In this activity, training tries dividing positions and keeps one with the fewest mistakes. The known labels tell us how each try did.

Count before choosing

  1. Put the fence at size three. Predict mango for every card larger than three.
  2. p4 and p5 are apples on the mango side. Count two mistakes.
  3. Move the fence to four. Now only p5 has the wrong name. Count one mistake.
  4. Move it to five. All five apples stay left; all five mangoes stay right. Count zero.
  5. Move it to six. p6 and p10 are mangoes on the apple side. Count two.
  6. Keep five rather than six because zero mistakes beats two on this collection.

Use the first slider to choose a fence you want to inspect. The lab also searches positions from zero to ten, half a centimetre apart. Each chart point reports the actual wrong guesses at that position. The lowest height marks the best count in this search.

There can be more than one winning position. Five and five-and-a-half both make zero mistakes on these cards. Our lab keeps the smaller position when counts tie. That is an agreed convention, not proof that five is nature's true dividing size.

After the search, the plot shows your chosen fence and the best fence with separate labels. If they overlap, the lines sit on top of each other. Read the variables to distinguish your count from the best count. The lab does not claim to search every imaginable kind of model.

The next card changes the question

Bring a fruit of size six and sweetness eight. Keep its true name covered. The saved size-five fence predicts mango. Only after writing that guess should you uncover its name. Checking a new fruit is different from choosing a fence using its answer.

Now imagine a size-six apple. The same fence predicts mango incorrectly. Adding this card gives the learner useful new evidence. It may also show that size alone cannot separate all fruit. More examples usually help when they represent the cases we will meet.

Repeating the same ten cards a hundred times adds no new kinds of fruit. Incorrect names can also teach the wrong rule. Quantity helps when it brings relevant variety and reliable observations. It cannot repair every bad assumption about the problem.

What this model can and cannot learn

A vertical fence always puts equal sizes on the same side. Give it an apple and mango with identical sizes. No position can name both correctly. The difficulty comes from the model's limits, not from failing to move the slider carefully enough.

A different model might also use sweetness. That could help if sweetness carries useful clues. We still need fresh examples to check it. A more complicated rule can create new mistakes as well as fix old ones.

Be the learner and the judge

Make the ten fruit cards from the table. Lay them on squared paper. One child moves a pencil fence; another writes predictions before turning cards over. A third counts mistakes. Swap jobs after three fence positions and compare the counts.

In the lab, put an apple on the right of the mangoes. Predict whether the best count can remain zero. Run the search and explain the result. Then make two differently labelled fruits share one size to test the fence's limit.

Ask your partner to choose a new card secretly. Agree on your fence before seeing its label. You have now separated improving a rule from checking its promise on something unfamiliar.

A low count needs context

Forgetting what happens on the line

A size-five fruit belongs to the apple side of our size-five rule. State that convention before counting. Otherwise partners can disagree while using the same fence.

Zero old mistakes means no future mistakes

Old cards only describe the cases already shown. Check new fruit before claiming the rule travels well. The size-six apple is a counterexample to a guarantee.

More copies mean more knowledge

Duplicate cards do not add fresh situations. Collect a broader, carefully named set. Investigate noisy labels instead of trusting a larger pile automatically.

A reason to move the fence

  • A threshold turns a measured size into a name prediction.
  • Known labels let you count wrong guesses.
  • Training compares possible rules using those counts.
  • Tied rules need a stated choice convention.
  • The best rule here is best only among the tried fences.
  • New examples can expose missing clues or a weak model.
