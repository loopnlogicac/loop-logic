Nana's mixed-up notebook

Nana writes a mango's size on one page. Its sweetness goes on another page. Later, Mina cannot tell which numbers belong together. She draws a box around each fruit's details. Tuktuk can finally follow the notebook without mixing two fruits.

Before a computer can learn from examples, we must organise them. One fruit should keep its measurements together. Its known name should stay attached to the correct fruit. A tidy table makes those connections visible.

You can use the same arrangement for a student or a house. The useful measurements change with the question. You would not measure a house's sweetness to predict its rent.

Here you will build a table and turn each row into a dot. You will see what a dot remembers and what it leaves out. No calculation beyond reading two numbers is needed.

One card has one job

An example is one item or event in our collection. Our example is one fruit. In a study task, one example could be one student's study record. Decide what a card represents before recording anything.

A feature is something we observe about an example. Size and sweetness are our two features. A feature must be available when we want to make the guess. A fruit's hidden name is not an available measurement for predicting that same name.

A label is the known answer we want the model to learn to predict. Here the label is apple or mango. Labels belong to a question: for another task, the answer could be ripe or unripe instead.

Read across p6. Its size is six, its sweetness is seven, and Nana named it mango. Read down the size column. Every entry answers the same kind of measurement question for a different fruit.

The card names p1 to p10 help us discuss rows. They are identifiers, not fruit measurements. A model using the card number could learn an accidental ordering rather than anything about fruit.

Carry one row onto squared paper

  1. Draw a horizontal size axis, from zero to ten centimetres.
  2. Draw a vertical sweetness axis, from zero to ten.
  3. Take p1. Move two spaces right and three spaces up.
  4. Place a dot there and give it the apple colour.
  5. Take p6. Move six right and seven up. Use the mango colour.
  6. Repeat for the remaining eight cards, keeping each row's two numbers together.

The lab reads one row, shows its two coordinates, and adds its dot. The original table stays beside the plot. At the end you should count ten dots and five fruits of each label. Overlapping dots on other inputs can look like fewer fruits, so also read the count.

Pause at p3. It shares size three with p2, but has sweetness four instead of two. Those dots belong above one another. Now find p6 and p10. They have size six, but different sweetness. Sharing one feature does not make two examples identical.

Try moving a clicked point to size four and sweetness eight. The dot belongs four spaces right and eight up. Changing its class changes its label colour, not its coordinates. A label is separate information attached to the point.

The dot has only two positions. Our fruit could also have weight and colour measurements. A two-axis picture cannot display all those features at once. Leaving them out of a picture does not mean they disappeared from the full record.

Change the question, then choose the columns

Suppose the question is whether a student passes an exam. Hours studied and attendance might be features. Passed or did not pass is the label. The final result should not be slipped into the features used to guess that result.

For a house, area and number of rooms might help predict monthly rent. Rent is then the answer column. A district can be an observation too, even though it is a name rather than a measurement. Later lessons explain how to represent such categories.

Not every table includes answers. A collection of toy sizes and colours may have no labels at all. We could still ask which toys look similar. Do not invent an answer column unless someone actually supplied that information.

Make a table that another person can read

Draw six paper fruit cards with size, sweetness and a fruit name. Ask a partner to copy them into a table. Exchange papers and check each row against its original card. Then plot the six points. Use imaginary fruit rather than cutting or tasting classroom fruit.

Keep a ruler label beside the size column. A value of six centimetres is different from six millimetres. Decide what the sweetness score means before using it. For our activity, it is a shared invented scale, not a health measurement.

Give your partner a card with sweetness left blank. Leave that cell empty and explain why. Writing zero would claim that you measured no sweetness. A missing measurement says something different.

Finally, make two fruits share both measurements but have different names. Their dots overlap. The plot now shows why these two features may be insufficient to identify every fruit. More careful observations may help, but a label cannot be recovered by wishing.

Protect the meaning of a row

Sorting one column alone

If you sort sizes without moving the rest of each row, mango names may attach to apples. Move complete rows whenever you rearrange the table.

Swapping the two axes

Size is horizontal in this picture. Read the axis labels before placing a point. Sweetness seven belongs on the vertical axis, even when the numbers look familiar.

Giving the answer as a feature

A model asked to predict a fruit name cannot already rely on that name. Cover the label while making a guess. Uncover it only to check the guess.

Read a card in both directions

  • One row keeps one example's observations together.
  • A column keeps the meaning and units of one observation.
  • Features are available clues for a prediction.
  • A label supplies the known answer for the chosen task.
  • Two features locate a point on two named axes.
  • Colours can carry labels without becoming coordinates.
  • Missing cells and overlapping points need careful interpretation.
