The robot chooses the same team

Tuktuk chooses runners for a school game. It learned from last month's lunchtime race cards. Mina notices that children who arrived by the late bus have no cards. The robot keeps overlooking them. Their missing examples are not evidence that they cannot run.

A model learns from the opportunities and mistakes inside its examples. If some children never get recorded, their abilities may stay invisible. A computer does not make a decision fair merely by putting a number beside it.

Fairness is about how people are treated and what effects a decision has. Privacy is about people having appropriate control over information about them. Both matter before collecting data, not only after a model goes wrong.

You will read a small selection story and compare two kinds of mistakes. You will then build questions for any smart app. People remain responsible for using the answers.

Look for the empty spaces in the records

Bias here means a skew in examples or decisions that can repeatedly disadvantage some people. It can enter through who is measured, how answers are assigned, or how predictions are used. It need not come from an openly unkind instruction.

Privacy concerns how personal information is collected, used and shared. A face photo or private message can reveal more than you intended. Hiding a name does not always make a record impossible to recognise.

Consent means informed permission for a stated use. Someone agreeing to join a classroom game has not agreed to public photo sharing. If children are involved, responsible adults must follow the relevant rules and protect their choices.

Questions that change a decision

1. Do the examples carry an unfair history?

Suppose past team leaders always picked friends. A model taught to copy those selections may repeat that preference. Even perfectly copying the old answers would not show that the new selection is fair. We need to question what the answers represent.

For the bus story, deleting the bus column does not create missing race times. Other clues could still track the same difference. Ask who had a chance to be observed. A fairer process might offer another race session and a way to appeal the result.

Checking group outcomes can reveal a problem worth investigating. Equal counts alone cannot prove fairness, and different counts alone do not explain the cause. We need the purpose, evidence and affected people's experience. No single score settles every fairness question.

2. Whose information is being used?

Mina wants to test a photo sorter. The safest class activity uses drawn faces. If she needed actual photos for a legitimate activity, she would first explain the purpose, access and deletion plan with a responsible adult. She would collect only what was needed.

A classmate can decline an optional activity without being mocked. Permission for private classroom use does not permit posting the pictures online. Copying them into another service changes who might receive them. Ask again about that different use rather than assuming permission travels everywhere.

Keep a plan for correcting or deleting records where appropriate. A promise that “the computer needs everything” is not a reason to collect everything. Often a smaller, less sensitive observation can answer the classroom question.

3. Which mistake hurts, and how?

A spam filter might hide an ordinary homework message. A person can miss a deadline. The app should offer a folder to inspect and a way to mark the mistake. It should not insist that every hidden message is unwanted.

A medical prediction can be much more serious. Missing a real illness can delay care. A false alarm can also cause worry and unnecessary investigation. A child should not diagnose anyone from a model score; qualified professionals need proper clinical evidence and responsibility.

Do not compare systems only by how many guesses they get right. Ask who experiences the wrong ones and how severe the consequences are. A useful safeguard reduces harm even when mistakes cannot be eliminated.

4. Who remains responsible?

The teacher who uses a team suggestion must explain and review it. The people building and operating the service also have responsibilities. “The model said so” is not an adequate answer to an excluded child.

A person reviewing a suggestion needs time, relevant information and authority to change it. Clicking approve without checking is not meaningful oversight. Keep a non-automated route available when the system cannot handle an unusual case.

Hold a paper app review

Give one group the six bus cards. Another group represents children whose races were never recorded. A third plays the teachers. Design a better selection process together. Use invented characters, and let everyone speak without sharing real school records.

Now review an imaginary homework-message app. Ask five questions: What does it predict? Whose examples taught it? What permission covers the data? Who is harmed by a wrong guess? How can a person challenge or correct the result?

Write an honest notice for the app: “This tool suggests possible spam. Check the review folder if a message is missing.” Avoid claiming that the tool never fails. Clear limits help people know when to look more carefully.

Shortcuts that do not protect people

Numbers must be neutral

Numbers inherit collection choices and old decisions. Investigate missing opportunities and questionable labels. Do not assume automation removes unfairness.

Permission for one thing covers everything

A classroom purpose and public sharing are different uses. Explain the actual plan before collecting information. Offer a less intrusive alternative when possible.

Blaming the model ends the discussion

People choose and operate the system. Name someone who can hear concerns and change decisions. A review process should be reachable by the people affected.

Keep people in the picture

  • Missing examples can hide missing opportunities.
  • Old answers can preserve unfair treatment.
  • Personal data needs a clear purpose and appropriate permission.
  • Collecting less can protect privacy.
  • Mistakes have different costs for different people.
  • Human review needs real authority to correct a decision.
  • Honest limits and appeal routes make tools easier to question.
