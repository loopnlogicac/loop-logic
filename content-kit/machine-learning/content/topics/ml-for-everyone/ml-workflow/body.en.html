How many baskets tomorrow?

Nana brings too many fruit baskets on a rainy morning. Several stay unsold. Mina proposes a machine that predicts everything. Nana asks one smaller question: “Should I prepare a busy-day basket tomorrow?” Rafi takes out a blank planning card. A clear question gives the project somewhere to begin.

A machine learning project is more than choosing a model. We must decide what help is useful, gather suitable examples and check the guesses. We also need a plan for mistakes after people start using it.

Think of cooking from a recipe. Excellent stirring cannot rescue the wrong ingredients. A model trained carefully on irrelevant examples can still answer the wrong question. Each stage affects the next.

Our whole project will fit on paper. You can carry out every stage with classmates. The aim is to learn the order and the reasons, not to build a real sales service.

A path with a return arrow

Ask a useful question
        ↓
Collect examples and check their meaning
        ↓
Set aside hidden checking cards
        ↓
Choose a rule and train on the practice pile
        ↓
Check on the hidden pile
        ↓
Use carefully, watch mistakes, return to the question

A workflow is an organised path through a task. The return arrow matters. A failed check can send us back to collecting examples or choosing a better question. Learning work rarely ends after one successful try.

A baseline is a modest starting guess used for comparison. Calling every day quiet is one possible baseline. A new model should earn its extra effort by doing something useful beyond that guess.

Monitoring means watching what happens after a system is used. A new school timetable or rainy season may change customers' habits. Earlier checks do not stay trustworthy forever.

Read the path before looking at a model. Where might a mistaken measurement enter? Where would you notice an unhelpful prediction? A workflow makes those responsibilities easier to name.

A busy-day basket project

1. Define a question someone can answer

Nana wants a busy or quiet prediction before opening the stall. Agree that busy means at least twenty customers during the morning. The guess helps prepare baskets, not judge customers. Record when the guess is made so later information cannot slip into the clues.

For this paper project, rain in the forecast and whether school is open are available clues. The actual number of morning customers is not available beforehand. It can provide the answer afterwards. If Nana instead needs an exact basket count, that would be a different task.

2. Collect and clean the cards

Use these six invented training mornings. Rain is recorded from the forecast before opening. School status is also known then. The busy label comes from the later customer count.

Before using a card, check what each field means. “School open” must use the same meaning on every card. If rain is missing, write unknown rather than pretending it was dry. Ask for a correction when possible; otherwise state how that card is handled.

Remove accidental copies of the same morning. Keep different mornings even when their clues match. A duplicated record and two genuinely similar days are different things. Use invented mornings in class so nobody needs to share private customer details.

3. Reserve checks, then choose and train

Put two additional morning cards aside before comparing rules. Their clues may be visible, but keep answers sealed. On the six training cards, an always-quiet baseline misses A, B and E. A rule saying busy when rain is not forecast misses none.

Choosing that rule from the six named mornings is our training step. School status was available, but the chosen rule did not need it. Not every recorded observation must be used. Keep a short note explaining which clues drive the decision.

4. Open the separate check

The two hidden cards are G, dry with school open and a busy answer, and H, rainy with school closed and a busy answer. Predict before opening their answers. The rain rule names G correctly but calls H quiet. It makes one mistake out of two.

The always-quiet baseline misses both G and H. Our rule beats it on this small check, but one missed busy day still matters. Nana could lose sales by preparing too little. Two cards are far too few to decide how well the rule will work across seasons.

5. Use with a fallback and keep watching

Suppose Nana tries the suggestion while keeping spare baskets available. He records predictions and later counts each morning. If several rainy mornings become busy, he reviews the pattern. A changed bus stop or school schedule might explain why the old rule stopped helping.

Nana stays responsible for preparation. He can override the suggestion when local knowledge matters. Improving the model may require broader observations, corrected records or a different question. The workflow returns to the stage that needs attention.

Run the whole recipe without a computer

Form a group of four: question keeper, card collector, learner and examiner. Write the six A–F mornings. The examiner keeps G and H answers hidden. State the decision time and the busy definition. Compare the baseline with a learned rule, write predictions, then reveal answers.

Finish by writing a use plan. Who sees the suggestion? Who can disagree? What mistake would make you pause the trial? Swap roles and introduce a missing forecast. Decide how to handle it before revealing the new day's outcome.

Do not improve the rule and then claim G and H are unseen again. For another final check, ask the examiner to invent fresh cards. Keep a record of which information guided each change.

Stages that are easy to skip

Starting with the fanciest model

A model cannot decide what Nana needs. Write the question, timing and useful action first. A paper baseline may already solve enough of the problem.

Using tomorrow's actual sales as today's clue

That information arrives too late for the decision. List only observations available before opening. Later outcomes belong to checking and future learning.

Stopping at a promising score

People's routines and conditions change. Assign someone to watch mistakes during use. A clear fallback lets Nana stop relying on a weak suggestion.

Keep the question connected to the action

  • Define what is predicted and when the guess is needed.
  • Collect relevant examples with consistent meanings.
  • Clean records without turning unknown values into facts.
  • Reserve checking answers before comparing rules.
  • Compare a learned model with a baseline.
  • Count useful successes and costly mistakes on fresh cases.
  • Monitor use and return to earlier stages when conditions change.

Before anyone relies on a suggestion, ask about fair, safe and honest machines.
