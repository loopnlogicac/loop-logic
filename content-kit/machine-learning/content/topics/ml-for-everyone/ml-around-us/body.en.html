The keyboard's surprising suggestion

Nana types “কাল সকালে” to Mina. His keyboard suggests “আসব”. He wanted to write “দোকান খুলব”. Mina laughs at the unfinished message. The keyboard offered a likely continuation. It did not read Nana's plan for tomorrow.

Machine learning often appears as a small guess inside a larger app. A keyboard may guess the next word. A map may guess travel time. Neither guess needs to be correct every time to be useful.

You cannot identify machine learning from a colourful screen alone. A fixed instruction can look clever too. Ask what the system predicts and what examples could have taught it. Sometimes the app does not tell us enough to know.

This lesson follows six familiar uses. You will practise separating a prediction from the action taken afterwards. You will also ask what happens when the prediction is wrong.

Find the guess inside the app

A recommendation is a suggestion about something you might choose. It could be a video or a notebook in a shopping app. A system may learn from earlier choices. A shop can also recommend a notebook through a fixed sale rule.

A signal is a clue available to the system. A click is a signal, but its meaning is uncertain. Mina may click a video by accident. Watching a clip does not prove she liked it.

Six everyday investigations

Videos and shopping

Imagine six viewing cards: three cricket clips, two drawing lessons and one cooking lesson. Mina finishes the drawing lessons but leaves the others quickly. A recommender might rank another drawing lesson higher. It learns a pattern in choices rather than a rule about Mina's whole personality.

A shopping recommender could use earlier baskets to suggest a pencil with a notebook. The suggestion may help, or it may tempt unnecessary spending. Repeated suggestions can also narrow what someone sees. Try searching for a different subject before assuming that the suggestions show everything available.

Face unlock and photo tags

Face unlock asks whether a new face image matches an enrolled person. Photo tagging asks which person might be in a picture. Both can use learned image patterns. They are different tasks, and an app may use additional checks beyond a model.

A shadow, a changed camera angle or an unfamiliar face can cause mistakes. Matching faces does not reveal someone's honesty, feelings or permission to share their photo. Do not collect classmates' faces for our card games. Draw pretend faces instead.

Traffic on a map

Rafi has ten earlier journey cards from a road in Dhaka. Five rainy trips took longer than five dry trips. A model could use rain, time and recent speeds to predict a delay. It is estimating what may happen, not guaranteeing when the bus will arrive.

A sudden road closure might be absent from those cards. Current reports and human judgement still matter. If a map displays a route, separate its travel-time prediction from its route-finding instructions. Many useful parts of a map do not require learning.

Spam and unusual payments

A spam filter can learn from messages already marked unwanted or ordinary. It predicts which kind a new message resembles. A class notice containing “prize” might look like spam. Always treating that word as proof would lose useful messages.

A mobile-banking system may learn patterns in normal payments and flag an unusual one. An alert means “check this”, not “this person is guilty”. A fixed spending limit may operate beside it. A person needs a way to review and correct a mistaken block.

Bangla next-word prediction

A keyboard may learn which words often follow “কাল সকালে”. It may suggest “আসব” because that continuation appeared in earlier text. Nana remains free to write “দোকান খুলব”. A frequent continuation is not the only sensible sentence.

Bangla spelling, regional wording and mixed English can change the clues. A keyboard trained on a narrow collection may suggest poorly for other writers. Avoid entering private messages into unknown services just to explore predictions.

Clever-looking actions without learning

Consider a calculator, an alarm and a light controlled by a timer. Arithmetic follows specified operations. The alarm follows its set time. The timer switches according to a schedule. None needs examples to choose these behaviours.

A timer product could contain a separate learned feature, such as predicting occupancy. Name the particular behaviour you are discussing. Calling every automatic action machine learning makes it harder to notice what the data actually affects.

Interview an imaginary app

Make five paper stations: videos, faces, roads, messages and words. At each station, one child offers an input card. Another child proposes a guess. A third asks where examples might come from. Use drawings and invented messages throughout the activity.

Add a sixth station for an alarm clock. Ask which station can follow a complete written instruction without learning. Then invent one mistake at each prediction station. Decide who could notice the error and what a useful correction would look like.

If you inspect a real app, read its explanation with an adult. Treat “may use learning” differently from a documented claim. You can say “I do not know” when the source of a suggestion is hidden.

When appearances mislead

One name for every component

An app contains many parts. Identify the prediction you mean, such as arrival time. A login button or payment receipt is not evidence of learning.

A click means love

People click for many reasons, including mistakes. Describe clicks as imperfect clues. Recommendations should let people change their preferences.

An alert becomes an accusation

Unusual behaviour can be harmless. Ask for review before treating a warning as a final judgement. The cost of an error matters to the person affected.

A useful question for any app

  • Look for a specific guess inside a service.
  • Recommendations can learn from uncertain signals.
  • Face matching and photo tagging answer different questions.
  • Traffic estimates may fail when circumstances change.
  • Spam and payment alerts need correction routes.
  • Bangla word suggestions offer possibilities, not intentions.
  • Fixed instructions can also produce helpful automatic actions.
