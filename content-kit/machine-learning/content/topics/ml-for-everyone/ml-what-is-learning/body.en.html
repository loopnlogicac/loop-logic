What changed after practice?

Mina sees a kitten sleeping beside Nana's stall. Next morning, a striped cat visits. She calls that one a cat too. Nana asks how she knew. Mina has never memorised a perfect cat definition. She remembers many cats, including drawings in her schoolbooks.

Learning helps us handle something we have not seen before. Mina recognises a new cat after seeing other cats. She may still mistake a toy for an animal. Learning does not promise that every guess will be right.

A computer can also use examples to improve its guesses. People choose its task and give it a way to practise. The computer changes a reusable rule by following instructions. It does not need to feel curiosity or understand a cat as Mina does.

By the end, you can explain this kind of learning. You will also tell a written instruction from a learned rule. Bring your own guesses; changing a guess is part of the work.

A model is a reusable pattern or rule built from examples. Think of a rule card inside Tuktuk's pocket. Training can change what is written on that card. Using the card on another picture gives a guess.

Machine learning means using examples to build a model that can make predictions on new cases. A prediction is the model's guess. It may name an animal or estimate tomorrow's visitors. Our animal cards are a small pretend task, not a complete animal recogniser.

Two routes to a fruit-sorting rule

Route one: Nana writes the instruction

Nana tells Tuktuk, “Put fruit larger than five centimetres in the mango basket.” Tuktuk follows that instruction on every fruit. People supplied the dividing point. Even if the rule works well, following it is not learning from examples.

Now picture a different route. Nana provides these six already named fruits. Size is measured in centimetres. Sweetness is a pretend tasting score from zero to ten. They are teaching cards, not a guide to identifying real fruit.

Route two: compare guesses with known names

  1. Tuktuk tries a size dividing point of three. Fruit larger than three is called mango.
  2. It checks every card against Nana's name. Card 3 is wrongly called mango.
  3. It tries a dividing point of five. All six names now match.
  4. It keeps this better rule for later fruit cards.
  5. Nana brings an unseen fruit of size seven. The saved rule predicts mango.

People still wrote the instructions for trying and comparing rules. The examples helped choose which rule to keep. That distinction separates learning from merely obeying Nana's first instruction.

The fifth step uses the learned model without changing it. Learning happens while the rule is being chosen or adjusted. A working model need not learn again after every prediction. Nana can put its rule card away and reuse it tomorrow.

Suppose tomorrow's size-seven fruit is actually an apple. The prediction is wrong, even though all six earlier cards matched. Six examples cannot show every possible fruit. We need fresh checks before trusting the rule beyond this tiny game.

Teach a classmate an unfamiliar category

Draw six invented creatures. Give three a dot on the belly and three a stripe. Secretly name the dotted family “Luma”. Show a partner four named cards. Ask them to name the two hidden cards. Then add a striped creature with ears like a Luma. Discuss which clue they followed.

Repeat the activity after swapping one known name by mistake. Your partner may learn the wrong clue from that card. Good teaching examples need careful answers. You can explain a surprising guess without blaming the learner for guessing.

Try writing an exact animal definition with your friends. Another friend should search for a counterexample, such as a toy or a sleeping cat. This shows why hand-written rules can become awkward. It does not show that examples solve every problem.

Claims to question

“The computer understands like a person”

A machine finding patterns is not evidence of feelings or human understanding. Describe the task it performs and the examples it uses. Avoid giving it powers that you have not tested.

“Every helpful program learns”

A bell ringing at a fixed time follows a written rule. It does not change from examples. Ask what part was selected from data before calling a program machine learning.

“Remembering the cards proves success”

Repeating known answers may hide a weak rule. Show an unfamiliar card and check its real name. Keep the distinction between a useful guess and a guaranteed fact.

What you can now explain

  • Examples give a learner cases to practise on.
  • A person may write a rule directly.
  • Learning can select a rule by comparing guesses with known answers.
  • A model stores a pattern that can be reused.
  • A fresh case can reveal a mistake that old cards missed.
  • Computers can learn patterns without having a human mind.
