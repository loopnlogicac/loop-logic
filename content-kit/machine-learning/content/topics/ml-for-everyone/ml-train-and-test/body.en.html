The practice book trick

Rafi reads a practice book with Tuktuk. The robot remembers every answer's position. Mina rearranges the questions. Tuktuk repeats the old sequence and fails. Rafi closes the answer page. “We need questions you have not memorised,” he says.

Correct answers on familiar examples do not prove a model handles new cases. We need to separate learning a rule from checking it. Otherwise the checking answers can quietly become part of the lesson.

Think of practice questions and a sealed exam envelope. You can learn from practice answers. You should not open the exam answers to improve your practice score. The separation gives the final check its meaning.

Here you will compare three rules on fruit cards. One ignores too much. One follows every training detail. One uses a broader pattern. The strongest performance on old cards may lose on hidden ones.

Two piles with different jobs

The training set is the collection used to build the model. Its answers can help choose the rule. The test set is a separate collection kept out of that choice. Its answers are opened after predictions have been fixed.

Overfitting means fitting training details so closely that the rule carries poorly to new examples. Memorising accidental noise can do this. A very flexible boundary can be perfect on practice cards and poor elsewhere.

The opposite problem is underfitting: a rule is too limited to capture useful patterns even in training. Calling every fruit apple ignores the size pattern. “Not studying enough” is an analogy; in a model the cause may be limited rule choices, not missing effort.

Lock a rule before opening the envelope

  1. Keep the first six cards for training. Put the last four cards in a sealed test pile.
  2. Build a simple dividing rule using training labels only.
  3. Build a flexible rule that follows the nearest training size.
  4. Build a flat rule that always predicts the most common training name.
  5. Write each rule's four test guesses while the test labels stay hidden.
  6. Open the labels, count mistakes separately, and compare the two piles.

The lab uses a deliberate first-cards/last-cards split for this paper exercise. Grey test dots have visible measurements but hidden labels during fitting. Its three tiny models use size alone. This is a transparent demonstration, not a claim that all real datasets should be split by row order.

The simple model tries midpoints between different training sizes, plus the ends of the axis. It keeps a threshold with the fewest training mistakes. Ties go to the larger threshold here. Its chosen dividing size is five. That names only p3 incorrectly in training.

The flexible model copies the label of the nearest training size. Equal distances use the earlier card. Its boundary switches back and forth around the isolated mango. It gets all six training cards right because each card is its own closest example.

The flat model calls every card apple. Three training apples and three training mangoes tie, so our convention chooses apple. It makes three training mistakes. It is too limited to reflect the broad difference between small apples and large mangoes.

Write predictions in ink

Make the ten cards above. One partner holds the last four labels face down. Another builds the three rules using six training cards. Write test predictions in ink before the first partner reveals answers. Draw separate mistake bars for each pile.

Choose a different number of test cards in the lab. Fewer training examples may change the learned fence and majority label. Predict which model might be most affected before replaying. Explain what information disappeared from training.

After seeing test mistakes, you may want to improve the rule. That is a reasonable next experiment. But those four cards are no longer a fresh final exam for the revised rule. Bring another hidden pile, or reserve a separate practice-check pile for choosing changes.

Keep the envelope honest

Moving the fence after seeing test answers

That uses test information to choose the model. Record the original result honestly. Use different unseen examples to assess a changed rule.

Choosing by the smallest training bar alone

The flexible model's zero training errors hide its two test errors. Compare performance on unfamiliar cards. Also ask whether those cards resemble future cases.

Complex always means bad

Some real patterns need flexible rules. Overfitting is about poor transfer, not the number of bends alone. Try suitable models and check them with separate evidence.

What the hidden pile tells you

  • Training examples help choose a model.
  • Test answers stay outside that choice.
  • Overfitting can produce a perfect practice score and weaker new guesses.
  • Underfitting misses useful structure even on familiar examples.
  • Counts need pile sizes and representative cases for context.
  • Revising from test answers uses up their freshness.
  • Four successful guesses cannot guarantee every later answer.
