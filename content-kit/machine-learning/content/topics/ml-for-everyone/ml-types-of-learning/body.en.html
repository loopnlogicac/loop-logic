Three games for Tuktuk

Mina brings Tuktuk three envelopes. The first holds named fruit cards. The second holds unnamed toy pictures. The third describes a maze with reward tokens. Tuktuk asks for the answers. “Only the first envelope has those,” Mina says. The three games offer different kinds of help.

Learning does not always come with a correct answer beside every example. Sometimes we receive names. Sometimes we search for groups. Sometimes an action earns a reward later. The available help shapes what we can learn.

These are three common learning setups. They describe the task and feedback, not the colour of the robot. The same object, such as a mango, can appear in different setups.

You will decide which setup fits a problem. You will also learn where text systems fit, without needing to build one. Start by asking what information the learner actually receives.

What is inside each envelope?

Compare the help, not just the objects

Maze playerPossible moves and rewards after actingChoose moves that bring more reward over time

Supervised learning learns from examples with known answers. Nana's named fruits fit here. The learner compares guesses with those names. A teacher need not sit beside the computer; the answers can already be recorded.

Unsupervised learning looks for structure without supplied answer labels. A toy sorter may group by shape or colour. More than one grouping can be useful. People must decide whether the groups help their purpose.

Reinforcement learning learns how to act using rewards from interaction. A maze player tries moves and notices what earns tokens. The useful feedback can arrive after several moves. A reward is a chosen signal, not proof that an action is good for everyone.

Read the available help first

A teacher supplies fruit names

Lay out six cards with size and sweetness. Three say apple: sizes two, three and four, with sweetness three, two and three. Three say mango: sizes six, seven and eight, with sweetness seven, eight and seven. Hide the name on a seventh card.

The task is to predict that hidden name using the six named cards. That makes it supervised learning. It does not matter whether the computer uses a fence or a different rule. The known names provide the teaching feedback.

Predicting tomorrow's number of visitors can also be supervised. Earlier days have both observations and actual visitor counts. The answer is a number rather than a fruit name. Having a numeric answer does not turn it into a different learning setup.

No one provides a group name

Now show six toys: two small red balls, two large blue balls and two red blocks. Give no labels. One child groups the balls together. Another groups all red toys together. Both used visible similarities.

The problem becomes unsupervised if the learner seeks groups without supplied answers. A grouping by shape may help pack a shelf. A grouping by colour may help an art activity. Ask the purpose before calling one arrangement wrong.

If Mina later labels pictures “ball” and “block” and asks Tuktuk to predict those names, the task changes. The same toys can now support supervised learning. The objects alone never decide the category of learning.

Actions earn feedback

Draw six squares leading around a wall toward a goal. A token is earned only when the player reaches the goal. The learner chooses moves, sees the new square and eventually receives feedback. A useful early move may earn nothing immediately.

This resembles reinforcement learning because actions affect later opportunities and rewards. A dog learning to sit for a treat is an everyday analogy. Real animal learning is richer than our maze game. We use the comparison to understand feedback, not to claim they are identical.

A learner that only chases the easiest token may miss a better later result. We must choose rewards carefully and keep exploration safe. In class, use paper squares; do not test reward-seeking behaviour near roads or people.

Where text models fit

Many large language models first learn through self-supervision. Parts of existing text provide clues and the next or hidden words provide training answers. People do not need to hand-name every word example. This is related to supervised prediction, with targets drawn from the data itself. Later training can use human feedback or reward signals. Predicting plausible words does not guarantee truth or understanding.

Consider “Mina put the book on the ...”. Hiding the next word creates a prediction task. Several continuations might be reasonable. A text system may learn common patterns without checking whether the described event happened.

Change the help, keep the cards

Use six drawn toys for three rounds. First reveal shape names and ask for a new name prediction. Next remove the names and ask for groups. Finally place toys on a paper maze and give a token only for reaching a goal. Describe what feedback changed between rounds.

Write three problem cards: naming a fruit, organising photos without names, and choosing game moves to earn points. Match each to an envelope. For every match, finish the sentence “The learner receives ...”. That explanation matters more than memorising a category name.

Try a tricky case: a shop prints a fixed reward voucher after every fifth visit. It has rewards, but no learner changes its behaviour from experience. Rewards appearing on a screen do not by themselves make reinforcement learning.

Words can hide the setup

Supervised means constant adult supervision

The supervision comes from known answers. A stored table can supply them. Identify the label rather than searching for a person beside the machine.

Unnamed groups have one official solution

Shape and colour can lead to different sensible groups. Judge the grouping against a stated purpose. Names added afterwards are human interpretations.

Every good answer is reinforcement learning

Checking a fruit's name is supervised feedback. In a reinforcement setup, actions influence what happens and rewards guide behaviour over time. Ask whether the learner is acting in an environment.

Choose by the feedback

  • Known example answers support supervised learning.
  • Finding structure without answer labels is unsupervised learning.
  • Actions and later rewards guide reinforcement learning.
  • The same objects can support several learning tasks.
  • A useful grouping depends on its purpose.
  • Self-supervised text training draws targets from text itself.
  • A plausible continuation can still describe something false.
