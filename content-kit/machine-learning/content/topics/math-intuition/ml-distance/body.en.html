A kite and a rickshaw choose different routes

A kite flies straight from Mina's rooftop to a friend's roof. Nana's rickshaw must follow two streets around a closed courtyard. Both journeys start and finish at the same places. Tuktuk reports two lengths and thinks one must be wrong. Mina draws the courtyard and shows why the travel rules matter.

Distance between examples also needs a rule. Two fruits can look close under size and sweetness, yet differ greatly under weight. Choosing measurements and their units is part of choosing what similarity means.

You will measure straight and block routes, calculate them with NumPy, and see a change of units reverse a nearest-example decision. Start by drawing the paths before reaching for a formula.

A diagonal or two sides

A Euclidean distance measures the straight route between coordinates. A Manhattan distance measures coordinate-aligned travel, like streets along a grid. A metric is a rule for distances that obeys consistent properties, including symmetry and the triangle inequality.

With endpoints one across and one up, then four across and five up, the horizontal change is three and the vertical change is four. Trace the horizontal segment with your finger. Then trace the vertical one. The diagonal joins the same ends without visiting the corner.

Neither route becomes negative when the return trip goes left and down. Direction belongs to the displacement. Distance keeps only its nonnegative size under the chosen rule.

Measure gaps before combining them

Let the first point be a and the second b. Write dx=b.x-a.x and dy=b.y-a.y. Pythagoras gives Euclidean distance sqrt(dx²+dy²). Manhattan distance is |dx|+|dy|, where vertical bars mean absolute value: ignore a negative sign when measuring the travel length.

For Q to A, dx=3 and dy=4. The squared lengths are nine and sixteen; their sum is twenty-five. Its square root is five. The block route is three plus four, giving seven. Squared distance twenty-five is useful for comparisons but is not the distance five.

For ranking candidates under Euclidean distance, comparing squared lengths gives the same order as comparing their square roots. The square-root function increases on nonnegative inputs. Report an actual length when someone asks how far apart the points are.

Match geometry and numeric code

The plain Python calculation names both signed changes. That helps you inspect the formula when an endpoint moves across the other endpoint.

The NumPy version treats the displacement as an array. The norm measures straight length; the sum of absolute entries measures the block route. Both methods retain the same coordinate order.

The library example reads two actual rows from the shared tiny fruit collection. It compares a direct NumPy calculation with scikit-learn's Euclidean pairwise distance. Agreement here verifies the arithmetic on the same features, not whether those features capture every useful similarity.

All three programs keep intermediate precision. Round the distance you show to a reader; rounding every squared component first can slightly change close comparisons.

A number can hide a unit choice

  • Adding signed gaps: three right and three down would cancel. Use squared gaps or absolute gaps according to the metric.
  • Forgetting the square root: twenty-five is the squared length of a three-by-four displacement. Its straight length is five.
  • Comparing unlike units without a reason: a gram-scale feature can dominate a small score. Document and choose scales before interpreting closeness.
  • Assuming close means the same label: distance sees only the selected features. More evidence may distinguish fruits with equal measurements.

Optional: geometry in a feature space

For d-dimensional vectors a and b, Euclidean distance is the square root of the sum of (aj-bj)² over all coordinates j. Manhattan distance sums |aj-bj| instead. Both take O(d) time per pair and can use O(1) extra running storage.

For two coordinate lengths u and v, (u+v)²=u²+2uv+v²≥u²+v². Therefore the block route is at least as long as the straight route. They tie when one coordinate gap is zero.

Scaling a feature by s multiplies its squared Euclidean contribution by s². Choosing scales therefore changes the geometry. Later, feature scaling will make that choice explicit in a prediction pipeline. A scaling rule learned from data must use training examples alone.

Name the route and the units

  • Subtract coordinates to get a displacement.
  • Pythagoras gives straight distance.
  • Absolute changes give block distance.
  • Reversing endpoints preserves both lengths.
  • Matching coordinates produce zero distance.
  • Measurement units can reverse a similarity ranking.
