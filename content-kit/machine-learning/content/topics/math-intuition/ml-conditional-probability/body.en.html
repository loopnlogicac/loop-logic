A red stamp on Nana's delivery cards

Nana stamps some delivery cards red when he suspects a damaged bag. Most bags are sound. Mina sees a red card and assumes its bag must be damaged. Tuktuk counts several sound bags with the same red stamp. The stamp is useful, but it does not tell the whole story. Mina decides to sort all the cards into a table.

New information changes which examples are relevant to a question. When you know a card is red, you should compare it with other red cards. Looking only at how often damaged bags receive red stamps answers a different question.

You will practise changing a denominator, check independence and apply Bayes' rule to a toy medical-test table. The medical numbers explain probability arithmetic; they are not estimates for an actual disease or test.

Given means keep only this group

A conditional probability describes a chance within a specified group. A prior is the chance before the new evidence. A posterior is the chance after using that evidence.

Keeping counts visible helps you resist a common reversal: a test often detecting illness does not by itself tell you how often a positive result means illness.

Build both rows before reading one column

Write P(A|B) for the probability of event A given event B. For a finite count table, divide the count in both A and B by the count in B. In probability notation, P(A|B)=P(A and B)/P(B), provided P(B)>0. The vertical bar means given, not division.

In the toy medical population, ten people are ill and nine hundred ninety healthy. Sensitivity is the positive-test share among ill people. With sensitivity 0.9, expect 10×0.9=9 true positives and one false negative. The healthy false-positive share is 0.1, giving 990×0.1=99 false positives and 891 true negatives.

A positive result restricts attention to 108 people. Only nine are ill. The posterior illness probability is 9/108, about 0.083. The prior was 10/1000=0.01, so the positive result raises the chance substantially while leaving most positive results in the healthy group.

healthy = 1000 - sick
TP = sick * sensitivity; FN = sick - TP
FP = healthy * falsePositive; TN = healthy - FP
positive = TP + FP
posterior = TP / positive

Increase the ill count to two hundred while holding both test rates fixed. Expected true positives become 180 and false positives eighty. The posterior is 180/260, about 0.692. The same test rates give a different answer because the prior illness share, also called its base rate, changed.

When multiplication produces 4.5 expected cases, interpret that as an average over repeated hypothetical populations. Do not round each cell to a person before calculating the ratio. An expected count is not a claim that a physical person can be divided.

Cover the irrelevant column

Start with the rare-illness preset. Predict whether raising the false-positive share will raise or lower the posterior. Replay and point to the cells responsible. Then choose no false positives: every positive belongs to the ill row under that special assumption.

Use ten counters for Nana's delivery table. Mark two damaged and eight sound. Give red paper slips to both damaged counters and two sound ones. Ask one classmate for the red share among damaged bags and another for the damaged share among red cards. Each classmate must say which counters form their denominator.

Make another table with forty people in group A and sixty in group B. Half of each group carries a bag. Different group counts can still have equal conditional bag probabilities.

Name the population of every fraction

  • Reversing the condition: write both denominators explicitly before comparing P(positive|ill) and P(ill|positive).
  • Ignoring the healthy population: even a modest false-positive rate can create many false positives when healthy people are numerous.
  • Rounding expected cells early: keep fractional expectations until the final displayed probability.
  • Calling different counts dependence: compare within-group proportions, not just raw bag totals.
  • Claiming a medical diagnosis: these invented rates teach arithmetic. Real interpretation needs appropriate clinical evidence and context.

Optional: reverse a condition with Bayes

Let I mean ill and + mean a positive result. The common joint probability can be written in two ways: P(I and +)=P(+|I)P(I)=P(I|+)P(+). Dividing by P(+) gives Bayes' rule: P(I|+)=P(+|I)P(I)/P(+).

The denominator includes both mutually exclusive groups: P(+)=P(+|I)P(I)+P(+|not I)P(not I). With the first preset it is 0.9×0.01+0.1×0.99=0.108. The numerator is 0.009, giving 0.009/0.108=1/12.

Independence means learning B does not change A's chance whenever conditioning is defined: P(A|B)=P(A). Equivalently, P(A and B)=P(A)P(B). In the complete bag table, P(bag|A)=20/40=0.5 and P(bag|B)=30/60=0.5, equal to the overall 50/100.

Equal proportions in a small sample need not establish independence in the wider population. Sampling variation, missing people and selection can change that conclusion. Distinguish an exact claim about a displayed population table from an inference about future data.

Building a two-by-two expected-count table uses constant arithmetic and storage. For many classes and pieces of evidence, conditional calculations require a model of their joint behaviour. Assuming independence can simplify that model, but the assumption needs to be stated and evaluated.

You can also reconstruct the prior by recombining conditional groups. In the medical table, illness occurs in nine of 108 positives and one of 892 negatives. Weight those illness shares by the probability of each test result. Their contributions are nine per thousand and one per thousand, which add back to ten per thousand. This is the law of total probability in count form. It checks that the rows and columns describe one consistent population rather than two unrelated samples.

Change the denominator when the information changes

  • Given selects a new comparison group.
  • Conditioning requires a nonzero denominator.
  • Prior and posterior refer to before and after evidence.
  • Bayes relates both directions of a conditional probability.
  • Base rates affect the positive posterior.
  • Independence concerns proportions within groups.
  • Expected fractional counts describe averages.
