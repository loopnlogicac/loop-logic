A ruler that both friends can agree on

Rafi and Mina draw different rulers through Nana's basket prices. Rafi likes a steep ruler. Mina likes one closer to the middle dots. Neither can settle the disagreement by pointing at a favourite basket. They need one rule that counts every mistake.

Simple linear regression learns a straight trend from one numeric feature. Simple here means one feature, not that real prices are simple. The target can still depend on many factors absent from the notebook.

Least squares gives both friends the same fitting rule. It adds the squared vertical gaps and chooses the line with the smallest total. You will calculate its slope and intercept without searching every possible ruler position.

The calculation uses averages and products you can do on paper. After seeing the gaps, work through five rows. Then compare your result with NumPy-style arithmetic and a fitted scikit-learn model.

Imagine a small square drawn on each vertical gap. Long gaps create much larger squares. A least-squares line makes the combined square area as small as possible. The intercept is the line's predicted height at input zero. A coefficient is a learned multiplier; with one feature it is the slope.

The line need not join the first and last dots. Those two dots are not entitled to decide everything. It also need not touch the middle observation. The fitting rule considers all five measurements with equal weight.

Start with a guessed slope and intercept. The lab draws its gaps, then computes the fitted line from centred sums. Moving the guessed line changes the opening picture but cannot change the final optimum for unchanged points.

Centre, multiply and divide

The feature mean is x_mean=(1+2+3+4+5)/5=3. The target mean is y_mean=(3+4+5+4+9)/5=5. Subtract each mean from its own column. Centring moves the cloud's middle to the origin without changing its shape.

Add the product column to obtain sxy=12. Add the squared-feature-gap column to obtain sxx=10. The fitted slope is w=sxy/sxx=1.2. Recover the intercept with b=y_mean-w*x_mean=5-1.2*3=1.4.

At x=4 the fitted value is 6.2, leaving residual -2.2. The five residual squares are 0.16,0.04,0,4.84,2.56. Their total is 7.6 and their average is 1.52. Keeping the large fourth gap is allowed because moving the ruler to fix it would worsen other gaps enough to raise the total.

find x_mean and y_mean
sxx = sum of squared centred x values
sxy = sum of centred x times centred y
if sxx is positive: slope = sxy / sxx
otherwise: choose slope = 0
intercept = y_mean - slope * x_mean

If every x equals 2, there is no horizontal spread. Dividing by sxx would divide by zero. Many slope/intercept pairs make the same prediction at x=2. The lab chooses zero slope and the target mean. That convention does not reveal how the target changes at another x.

Detect a constant feature

The final example states its convention instead of dividing by zero. A constant training feature cannot teach a response to changes that never occurred in training.

Check the denominator and the units

  • Using uncentred cross-products: that generally describes a different fit. Subtract the matching column mean first.
  • Forgetting the intercept: after calculating the slope, restore the original target height with y_mean-w*x_mean.
  • Calling slope importance: changing hours to minutes changes the coefficient's numeric size. State both units.
  • Deleting unusual observations automatically: least squares is sensitive to large gaps. Investigate their cause before changing the data.

Optional: why the formula minimises squared error

Let S(w,b) be the sum of (y_i-w*x_i-b) squared. Differentiating with respect to b and setting the result to zero gives b=y_mean-w*x_mean. Substituting that relation leaves the centred residual dy_i-w*dx_i.

The remaining derivative is -2 times the sum of dx_i*(dy_i-w*dx_i). Setting it to zero gives w*sxx=sxy. When sxx is positive, the quadratic curvature is positive and the slope is uniquely determined. If sxx is zero, the coefficient direction is unidentifiable.

Computing both sums takes O(n) time and O(1) extra working memory apart from stored observations. No assumption about normal residuals is needed to calculate the least-squares minimiser. Distribution assumptions become relevant when you want confidence intervals or hypothesis tests.

A fitting rule built from means

  • Least squares considers every squared vertical residual.
  • Centre both columns before calculating their cross-products.
  • Divide sxy by sxx to obtain the fitted slope.
  • Recover the intercept from the two column means.
  • A constant feature needs an explicit nonunique-fit convention.
  • A coefficient describes an association in stated units.
