The ruler misses the middle of the arch

Mina measures a toy cricket ball at several moments during its flight. A straight ruler misses the arch. Tuktuk replaces it with a ribbon that passes through every pencil mark. The ribbon now wiggles sharply between marks. Rafi asks which shape would predict the next photograph better.

Polynomial regression fits curved trends by adding powers of numeric inputs. Increasing the degree makes more shapes possible. That flexibility can capture a real pattern or chase accidental measurement differences.

The important comparison is between training and unfamiliar observations. A curve that fits every training dot may still predict badly between those dots. A straight line may be too restrictive even when it behaves smoothly.

You will fit degrees 1, 3 and 12 to a controlled curved example. You will also use a pipeline to create powers without losing the separation between fitting, selection and final evaluation.

Look between the training dots, not only at them.

A polynomial feature is an input power or product used as a new column. The degree is the highest total power allowed. Overfitting happens when fitting details of the training observations harms performance on unfamiliar examples.

Underfitting is the opposite restriction: the model cannot capture an important pattern. Both training and validation error can remain high. These labels describe performance relative to a problem and data collection, not moral qualities of a model family.

The lab fits actual polynomial least squares using reorthogonalised QR. Powers dependent on earlier columns are omitted. It compares degrees 1 through 12 on training dots and a fixed noiseless cubic validation rule. If you edit the training cloud, the validation rule stays fixed; it is a disclosed teaching assumption.

A degree-three prediction is b+w1*x+w2*x²+w3*x³. Powers bend the curve, but each weight still multiplies a fixed column. The model remains linear in the learned weights. Fitting therefore uses the same least-squares ideas as multiple regression.

For a clean hand example, use b=2,w1=1,w2=-2,w3=1. At x=0.5 the prediction is 2+0.5-2*0.25+0.125=2.125. At x=-1 it is 2-1-2-1=-2. Calculate the other three values before reading the larger demonstration.

The executable teaching example uses fifteen evenly spaced inputs from -1 to 1. Its targets follow the cubic rule plus a fixed oscillating disturbance. Thirty separate inputs have clean cubic targets. This construction lets you see what fitting noise does without claiming real measurements have a noiseless answer.

Degree 1 leaves broad curvature unexplained. Degree 3 captures the intended shape while averaging much of the disturbance. Degree 12 gains enough freedom to chase more noise. Read the actual validation minimum in the chart rather than assuming the true generating degree must always win a finite sample.

Nested polynomial feature sets cannot increase the exact optimal training squared error when another degree is added. Small numerical differences can still appear in finite-precision solvers. Validation error has no corresponding monotonic guarantee.

Select degree on study records

The course-data example reserves a final test partition first. It then splits development rows into training and validation. Median imputation, scaling and polynomial expansion stay inside each fitted candidate pipeline.

The comparison considers degrees 1,3,12. Only validation MSE decides the winner. After selection, the program reports one final test MSE for that fitted model. It does not compare final test scores and choose again. Another valid workflow would refit the chosen configuration on all development rows before that one evaluation.

Scaling before high powers keeps numeric magnitudes more manageable. It cannot guarantee a well-conditioned degree-twelve problem. Examine rank, warnings and held-out behaviour if features repeat or span a very narrow range.

Do not reward memorisation alone

  • Choosing the lowest training error: added powers can fit accidental noise. Compare candidates on validation rows.
  • Using the final test as a tuning dashboard: repeated choices adapt to those answers. Reserve fresh evaluation after model selection.
  • Calling every polynomial nonlinear in its parameters: the inputs have nonlinear powers, but the learned coefficients still enter linearly.
  • Duplicating the intercept column: either provide the bias column to a direct solve or let the regressor fit an intercept.
  • Trusting extrapolated curls: high powers can explode outside the training interval. Limit the claimed use range.

Optional: capacity, rank and conditioning

A one-feature degree-d polynomial has d+1 coefficients when an intercept is included. With fewer independent input locations, those coefficients need not be identifiable. Different coefficient vectors can match the same training values while disagreeing elsewhere.

The lab uses twice-reorthogonalised modified Gram-Schmidt to solve a bounded monomial design. A column with remaining norm below 1e-10 is treated as dependent and omitted. This is an explicit numerical rank policy, not an added statistical penalty. Default data have enough distinct locations for every shown degree.

Normal equations square the design matrix's condition number, which can amplify roundoff problems. QR or singular-value decomposition is preferable for unregularised fitting. Scaling the input interval and using orthogonal polynomial bases can also improve numerical behaviour without changing the intended polynomial function space.

Model flexibility trades approximation error against sensitivity to training fluctuations. This is related to the bias-variance tradeoff. The finite validation winner can differ from the data-generating degree because the score itself has sampling variation.

For several original features, PolynomialFeatures can add interactions such as x1*x2 as well as powers. The number of columns rises rapidly with feature count and degree. Estimate that cost before constructing a large expansion, and keep feature selection or degree tuning within development data.

A flexible fit needs an honest comparison

  • Polynomial columns let linear weights create curved predictions.
  • The highest input power is the polynomial degree.
  • A restricted straight line can underfit curved structure.
  • A high-degree curve can chase measurement disturbances.
  • Validation error chooses complexity before final testing.
  • Rank and numerical conditioning matter for high powers.
