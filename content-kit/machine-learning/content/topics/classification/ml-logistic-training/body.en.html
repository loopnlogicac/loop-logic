Correct a confident mistake more strongly

Tuktuk calls a known apple a mango with great confidence. On another card it hesitates near half-and-half. Mina wants the first mistake to matter more during learning. Rafi suggests a loss that asks how much probability the model gave the answer that was actually observed.

Log loss teaches a probability model through that distinction. A confident correct answer receives a small penalty, uncertainty receives a larger one and a confident wrong answer receives a large one. Gradient descent then adjusts weights and bias to reduce the average penalty.

You will calculate a batch update, inspect the moving boundary and compare a direct implementation with a library solver. The lab also includes a learning rate that diverges. Finishing a fixed number of steps and learning a useful model are separate claims.

A loss measures how undesirable a prediction was under a chosen rule. A gradient points toward increasing loss in parameter space. A learning rate controls how far an update moves against that direction. A long step can miss the valley rather than reach it sooner.

The table explains why a wrong answer at probability 0.01 matters much more than an uncertain answer at 0.5. The model should not receive the same correction for both. These penalties describe observed labelled examples, not a guarantee that future examples follow the same pattern.

Average each correction before changing parameters

Use six centred two-feature cards: (-2,-1), (-1,-2), (-1,-1) have label zero, while (1,1), (1,2), (2,1) have label one. Begin the paper calculation at zero weights and bias. Every score is zero and every probability is one-half.

For each row, subtract its label from the probability. Multiply that error by feature one for its first-weight contribution and by feature two for its second-weight contribution. Average each column over the six rows. The two feature gradients are both minus two-thirds; the bias gradient is zero because the classes balance.

At learning rate 0.3, subtracting the gradient gives weight 0.2 for each feature and leaves bias zero. Calculate every gradient from the old parameters before assigning any new coefficient. Changing the first weight and using it while computing the second would no longer be the stated batch step.

Why squared probability error is awkward here

Mean squared error can be applied to probabilities, but differentiating it through a sigmoid introduces the sigmoid slope p(1−p). Near zero or one, that factor is small. A confidently wrong prediction can then receive a weak correction. Binary cross-entropy with the sigmoid simplifies its score derivative to p−y.

Log loss also comes from the likelihood of observed binary labels under the model. A score-domain expression uses max(score,0) minus label times score plus log1p(exp(−abs(score))). It avoids taking log of a probability rounded to zero and avoids an overflowing exponential. Stable arithmetic is preferable to hiding warnings.

The library solver and direct batch method need not take the same path. They agree at the same rounded optimum because the data, objective scaling and intercept policy match. Comparing different penalty strengths while expecting identical coefficients would be a comparison of different problems.

Optional: the score gradient and regularisation

For binary label y and sigmoid probability p, cross-entropy is −y log(p)−(1−y)log(1−p). Differentiating with respect to the linear score gives p−y. The feature-weight gradient is its mean product with that feature; bias uses the mean error alone.

Adding strength times ||w||²/2 adds strength times w to the weight gradient. This convex objective with a positive L2 weight penalty has well-controlled weight directions, although a poor gradient-descent step size can still fail numerically. Algorithmic convergence and statistical generalisation answer different questions.
